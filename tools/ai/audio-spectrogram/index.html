What This Tool Shows

A spectrogram plots time left to right, frequency bottom to top, and loudness as colour: black is silent, red and yellow are loud. Speech, music and noise each leave a recognisable pattern. The part that matters most for spotting synthetic or re-encoded audio is the top edge: where the energy stops.

This viewer decodes the file with your browser's Web Audio API and draws 1,024 time slices. Each slice is a 512-point FFT with a Hann window, shown on an 80 dB colour scale with a linear frequency axis from 0 Hz up to the Nyquist limit labelled in the top-left corner.

How to Use the Audio Spectrogram Viewer

  1. Choose or drop an audio file. WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC, AAC and M4A work if your browser can decode them. The file isn't uploaded.
  2. Read the top edge of the image. Find the height where the colour fades to black and estimate its frequency using the labels: the top of the image is the Nyquist value shown, and the middle line is half of it.
  3. Play it back. Use the audio player to match what you hear (breaths, pauses, background noise) with what you see.
  4. Export the image. "Export Spectrogram (PNG)" saves the picture for a report. "Load Other File" clears the view.

The viewer always draws 1,024 slices, however long the file is. On a 3-second clip each slice is about 3 ms apart; on a 1-minute clip they're about 60 ms apart, so short details get skipped. Trim long recordings to the part you care about first.

Reading the Cutoff: What Different Sources Look Like

Digital audio can only contain frequencies up to half its sample rate. Many audio sources also filter off the top on purpose. The height where energy stops is therefore a clue to where the audio has been:

Source Typical top edge Why
Traditional phone call (narrowband)About 3.4 kHz8 kHz sample rate and a voice-band filter
HD Voice / wideband callsAbout 7 kHz16 kHz sample rate
Many neural text-to-speech systems8, 11 or 12 kHzModels are often trained and run at 16, 22.05 or 24 kHz
MP3 or AAC at common bitratesRoughly 16 to 20 kHzEncoders low-pass the signal to save bits; lower bitrates cut lower
Uncompressed recording at 44.1 or 48 kHzUp to about 20 kHz, often fading into noiseNo filter except the microphone and converter

These ranges overlap, which is the main limit of this method. A hard edge at 11 kHz in a "studio recording" is worth questioning, but the same edge appears when real speech is saved at 22.05 kHz. A voice clone that has been sent through WhatsApp or a phone call takes on that channel's cutoff instead of its own. Newer speech models that generate at 44.1 or 48 kHz can show energy all the way up.

Other Things to Look For

  • Breaths and mouth noise. Natural speech usually has soft broadband breaths between phrases. Missing or identical-looking breaths can be a clue in a long recording.
  • Background noise during pauses. A real room keeps a faint noise floor between words. Pure black gaps suggest generated audio or heavy noise suppression, which many calling and meeting apps also apply.
  • Splices. A sudden change in the noise floor or top edge partway through can mean audio from two sources was joined.
  • Harmonics. Voiced speech shows stacked horizontal bands. Smeared or unusually regular bands can be a sign of a vocoder, but at this tool's resolution (about 86 Hz per FFT bin at 44.1 kHz) the difference is often hard to see.

None of these proves a recording is real or fake. If a voice message asks for money, passwords or urgent action, verify it through a separate channel, such as calling the person back on a number you already have.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a spectrogram prove that a voice is AI-generated?
No. It shows clues, such as a hard frequency cutoff or missing background noise, but compression, phone networks and noise suppression produce the same patterns in real recordings. Use it alongside the file's origin and metadata, and confirm important requests through another channel.
Why does the Sample Rate always show 44100 or 48000 Hz?
Browsers decode audio at the audio device's sample rate, so a 16 kHz or 22.05 kHz file is resampled before the tool sees it. The original rate still shows up in the image as an empty band above half the original rate.
Why does an MP3 show a cutoff around 16 to 20 kHz?
MP3 and AAC encoders usually remove the highest frequencies to save space, and the lower the bitrate the lower the cutoff. That edge comes from compression, not from voice cloning, so expect it in most downloaded or shared audio.
Which channel is shown for stereo files?
Only the first (left) channel is analysed and drawn. If the channels differ, for example in a two-person call recording, export each channel as a separate file and open them one at a time.
Is my audio file uploaded?
No. The file is read and decoded in your browser with the Web Audio API, and the tool makes no network requests with it. Very large files may be slow or fail to decode, depending on your device's memory.
