Mathematical Deep-Dive: Core Stylometric Dimensions

Large language models generate text using autoregressive token sampling governed by probability distributions. At each step, the model computes logits across its vocabulary (e.g., 100,000+ tokens) and selects the next token using sampling strategies like Top-P (nucleus sampling) and temperature. This mathematical architecture leaves characteristic statistical fingerprints across four primary dimensions.

1. Type-Token Ratio (TTR) & Lexical Richness

Type-Token Ratio measures vocabulary diversity by calculating the ratio between the number of unique word stems (types, $V$) and the total word count (tokens, $N$): 

$$\text{TTR} = \frac{|V|}{N}$$

In natural human prose, authors employ varied synonyms, specialized adjectives, and idioms, resulting in higher lexical diversity. In contrast, unprompted LLMs default to "safe," high-probability transition words. Unless prompted for literary creativity, an LLM adhering to standard conversational instructions tends to repeat corporate boilerplate and explanatory crutches, depressing its TTR across long passages.

2. Shannon Information Entropy

Derived from Claude Shannon's seminal 1948 paper A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Shannon entropy ($H$) measures the average uncertainty, surprisal, or information density per character within a text string: 

$$H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i)$$

Where $P(x_i)$ is the empirical probability of character $x_i$ appearing in the text. Highly predictable, repetitive prose yields low Shannon entropy because subsequent characters can be anticipated with high statistical confidence. Human writing containing complex punctuation, diverse syllable structures, and varied vocabulary generates higher character entropy (typically exceeding 4.2 to 4.6 bits per character in English prose).

3. Burstiness and Sentence Cadence Dynamics

Burstiness is perhaps the most reliable heuristic differentiator between unedited machine generation and authentic human authorship. It evaluates the variation in sentence length and syntactic complexity across a body of text.

Human thought is inherently bursty. A human author might write an abrupt, three-word fragment. Then, they might follow it with a sprawling, 42-word periodic sentence punctuated by em-dashes, dependent clauses, and parenthetical asides. Language models, conversely, are trained with RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) to optimize for clarity, coherence, and helpfulness. As a consequence, unprompted models generate remarkably uniform sentences—hovering almost invariably between 15 and 25 words.

The BitDark profiler computes burstiness using the Coefficient of Variation (CV) of sentence lengths: 

$$\text{CV} = \frac{\sigma}{\mu} = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}(L_j - \mu)^2}}{\mu}$$

Where $L_j$ represents the word count of sentence $j$, $\mu$ is the mean sentence length, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. A burstiness score below 0.35 indicates mechanical uniformity; scores above 0.65 reflect dynamic human cadence.

4. Perplexity Proxy & Unigram Branching Factors

True neural perplexity requires running the text through an inference pass on a multi-billion parameter model (such as LLaMA or Mistral) to extract the exact cross-entropy loss: 

$$\text{PPL}(W) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ln P(w_k \mid w_1, \dots, w_{k-1})\right)$$

Because running a 70B parameter model locally in browser JavaScript is computationally impractical, the BitDark profiler utilizes an empirical Unigram Branching Proxy. It evaluates word-level cross-entropy based on empirical token distribution frequencies, calculating the effective branching factor. Lower values correlate with texts that follow constrained, predictable vocabulary channels.

Common Mistakes and Forensic Misconceptions

Investigators, educators, and editors frequently commit severe procedural mistakes when evaluating potentially synthetic text:

  • The Non-Native Speaker False Positive Trap: Non-native English writers often rely on simpler vocabularies, standardized transitional phrases, and grammatically conservative sentence lengths. Commercial detectors routinely misclassify non-native writing as AI-generated. Never penalize a writer based solely on statistical uniformities.
  • The Technical Documentation Distortion: Legal contracts, API documentation, medical case studies, and engineering specifications must use standardized terminology. By definition, a legal lease agreement will feature low Type-Token Ratios and repeated multi-word trigrams (e.g., "pursuant to section," "the tenant shall"). This is a hallmark of professional precision, not machine synthesis.
  • Testing Short Snippets: Pasting a single 20-word sentence into an NLP analyzer produces mathematically invalid results. Standard deviation and variance calculations require a sample size of multiple sentences to establish a stable mean.
  • Believing Simple Paraphrasers Defeat Linguistic Profiling: While commercial tools like QuillBot can scramble tokens to fool brittle neural classifiers, they often leave sentence cadence (burstiness) and low information entropy completely intact, remaining clearly visible on stylometric telemetry.

Frequently Asked Technical Questions

What is the difference between perplexity and burstiness?
Perplexity measures how predictable words are based on prior context—a measure of vocabulary and transition likelihood. Burstiness measures the variation in sentence lengths and cadences across a passage. AI models typically exhibit both low perplexity (predictable word choices) and low burstiness (monotonous, evenly spaced sentence lengths).
Why does unedited AI writing tend to have low burstiness?
During training and RLHF alignment, language models are incentivized to generate clear, balanced, and grammatically complete prose. As a result, the autoregressive generation loop gravitates toward a comfortable average sentence length (typically 15 to 25 words), avoiding the jarring structural contrasts common in human writing.
What is a good burstiness score for natural human writing?
A burstiness score (coefficient of variation of sentence lengths) above 0.50 is typical for engaging human prose. Exceptional creative writing, investigative journalism, and speech transcripts frequently reach scores between 0.60 and 0.90 due to intentional shifts between rapid dialogue, punchy statements, and elaborate descriptive clauses.
What does Shannon Entropy tell me about a text sample?
Shannon Entropy quantifies the information density in bits per character. Texts that repeatedly reuse the same character patterns or formulaic phrasing have lower entropy. Rich prose incorporating varied syntax, broad vocabulary, and expressive punctuation generates higher entropy values (usually above 4.3 bits/character in English).
Why does BitDark run this tool client-side instead of in the cloud?
Privacy and operational security. Uploading unpublished manuscripts, legal filings, proprietary source code, or internal corporate memos to third-party AI detection servers creates severe data leakage risks. By executing entirely in your browser's local memory, BitDark guarantees zero network egress.
Can an author deliberately bypass stylometric profiling?
Yes. A skilled prompter can instruct an LLM to "vary sentence lengths wildly between 3 and 40 words, avoid common transition words, and adopt an idiosyncratic vocabulary." This directly modifies the mathematical distribution of the generated text, demonstrating why statistical profiling can never serve as definitive legal proof.
What are N-grams and why do they matter in AI detection?
An N-gram is a contiguous sequence of n items from a given sample of text. Bigrams are two-word chains (e.g., "artificial intelligence") and trigrams are three-word chains (e.g., "in today's world"). High frequencies of formulaic N-grams are strong indicators of automated template generation or hallucination loops.
