⚠️ Limitations & Disclaimer

Sending a signal is not the same as being obeyed. This page reports what your browser is transmitting; it cannot tell you what any site does on receipt, because that happens on their systems and is invisible from here. GPC carries legal weight in some jurisdictions and none in others, so the same signal can be binding or merely advisory depending on where you and the site are. Do Not Track is effectively dead \u2014 it was never binding, the industry declined to honour it, and browsers have removed the setting. Treat an absent signal as something to enable and a present signal as a request made, not a protection confirmed. Everything is read locally and nothing is transmitted. Results are provided as-is; BitDark.net is not liable for decisions made from them.

What This Tool Does

Two opt-out signals have been attempted on the web, and your browser may be sending one, both or neither. This page reads them and tells you which — along with what each actually asserts, because they are not the same claim.

Global Privacy Control says you are exercising a right to opt out of the sale or sharing of your personal information. Do Not Track said you would prefer not to be tracked. The first is written to match the language of privacy statutes; the second was a polite preference, and that difference is the whole story of why one works and the other does not.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Read the result on load. The audit runs automatically and reports each signal's current state.
  2. If GPC is absent, enable it. Some browsers include it in privacy settings; others need an extension. It is worth turning on wherever you can, because it costs nothing and is binding in some jurisdictions.
  3. Click Re-audit Signals after changing a setting to confirm the change took effect. Settings that appear enabled but do not produce a signal are not unheard of.
  4. Note that DNT is likely to be absent and that this is expected, not a fault. The feature has been retired by most browsers.
  5. Click Export Signal Audit JSON if you want a record — useful when documenting a browser configuration for a team or a compliance review.
  6. Test each browser and profile separately. The setting does not follow you; a browser you use occasionally may be sending nothing.

How the Signals Travel

GPC is sent two ways. As an HTTP request header it accompanies requests, so a server sees it before any script runs. In JavaScript it appears as a property on the navigator object, which lets a page check it when deciding what to load. DNT worked the same way with its own header and property.

Both are trivially visible to any site. That visibility is deliberate — a signal nobody can read is useless — but it means the signal is also one more property that distinguishes your browser, which is the small cost discussed in the FAQ.

Why GPC Has Force and DNT Did Not

DNT arrived as a voluntary standard with no definition of compliance and no consequences for ignoring it. Advertisers were being asked to give up revenue in exchange for nothing, and predictably most declined. Without adoption it became noise, and browsers eventually dropped it.

GPC was designed around that failure. It is deliberately worded to match statutory opt-out language, so that where a law grants a right to opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information, a GPC signal can be recognised as a valid exercise of that right — which several jurisdictions, led by California, now do. That makes ignoring it a regulatory matter rather than a choice, and regulators have in fact acted on it. Outside those jurisdictions the signal is advisory, and whether a global company applies it everywhere or only where required is a policy decision you cannot observe.

A Worked Example You Can Reproduce

Run the audit and note the result. Frequently Asked Questions

Does sending GPC mean sites must respect it?
Only where law says so. GPC is a technical signal with no enforcement of its own; what gives it force is legislation that recognises it as a valid opt-out, which several jurisdictions now do. Outside those places a site may honour it voluntarily or ignore it entirely, and you cannot tell which from your side.
Why did Do Not Track fail?
It asked for cooperation and never got it. DNT had no legal backing, no agreed definition of what honouring it meant, and no way to verify compliance, so most of the industry declined. Browsers have since removed the setting from their interfaces. GPC exists because the lesson was that a signal needs a legal hook.
Can a site verify that I sent GPC, or can I verify that it obeyed?
The site can see the signal easily — it arrives as a request header and is readable in JavaScript. You cannot verify the reverse. What a site does with your data after receiving the signal happens on their systems, invisibly to you. The only external checks are regulatory enforcement and the site's own disclosures.
Does the signal make me more identifiable?
Slightly, in principle: it is one more bit that distinguishes you from users who do not send it. In practice the population sending GPC is now large enough that it adds little against the dozens of other properties a page can read, and the opt-out is generally worth more than the bit costs. The fingerprint audit shows the surrounding context.
Is anything sent to a server from this page?
No. The signals are read from your browser's own properties and displayed here. Export Signal Audit JSON writes a local file. Nothing is transmitted.
