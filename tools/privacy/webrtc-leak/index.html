What This Tool Does

The test creates a peer connection, the same object a video-calling site would, and collects the ICE candidates the browser produces. Each candidate is an address the browser believes it might be reachable at, and each one is visible to the page that asked. The results show which categories appeared and what each reveals.

That is the entire mechanism behind the WebRTC leak: no exploit, no vulnerability, just a page using a standard API for something other than making a call.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Click Initiate WebRTC Leak Test and let candidate gathering finish. It takes a few seconds.
  2. Look for host candidates. If you see an address ending in .local, mDNS obfuscation is working and your real local address stayed private. A literal address such as 192.168.1.x means it did not.
  3. Look for a server-reflexive candidate. This is your public address as seen by a STUN server. Compare it to what you expect: behind a VPN, it should be the VPN's address, not your own.
  4. Re-test with your VPN connected and disconnected. Comparing the two runs is the only reliable way to know whether your particular configuration leaks.
  5. Re-test after changing networks — a different Wi-Fi network, a tethered connection, a new VPN server. Behaviour varies with the interface set.
  6. Click Reset Diagnostics to clear the results from the page.

Why WebRTC Gathers Addresses

WebRTC connects browsers directly to each other, which is what makes low-latency calling possible. Direct connection is hard, because both parties are usually behind routers that perform address translation and do not accept unsolicited inbound traffic.

ICE solves this by gathering every possibility. Host candidates are addresses on local interfaces. Server-reflexive candidates come from asking a STUN server what public address your traffic appears to originate from. Relay candidates route through a TURN server when nothing direct works. Both sides exchange their lists and test combinations until one succeeds. Collecting addresses is not a side effect; it is the design.

How It Became a Leak, and How mDNS Fixed Most of It

A page can create a peer connection and read the candidates without ever placing a call, so for years any site could silently learn your internal network address and your public address. The internal one was valuable for fingerprinting and for mapping home networks; the public one defeated VPNs when the browser gathered a candidate over an interface outside the tunnel.

Browsers responded by replacing the real local address with a randomly generated .local name that resolves only on your own network. A peer on your network can use it; a remote page learns only that an interface exists. The public address side is harder, because WebRTC genuinely needs it to connect, so the remaining defence is network configuration: a VPN that routes every interface through the tunnel and blocks anything outside it leaves nothing for WebRTC to find.

A Worked Example You Can Reproduce

Run the test with no VPN and note the server-reflexive candidate — that is your public address, and it should match what any address-lookup site reports. Note the host candidates too, and whether they are .local names or literal addresses. Now connect your VPN and run it again. The public candidate should change to the VPN's address; if your own appears anywhere in the results, your configuration is leaking and the fix is in the VPN settings, not the browser. Finally, run the test twice in a row without changing anything and compare the .local names: they differ, because they are regenerated, which is why they cannot be used to track you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an mDNS candidate and why do I see one?
Modern browsers replace your real local IP address in ICE candidates with a randomly generated name ending in .local, resolvable only on your own network. A page therefore learns that you have a local interface without learning its address, and the value changes periodically. Seeing these means the protection is working.
Does WebRTC reveal my IP address through a VPN?
It can, depending on how the VPN is configured. The classic leak occurs when the browser gathers a candidate over an interface outside the tunnel, so the page sees an address the VPN was supposed to hide. A VPN that routes all traffic and blocks traffic outside the tunnel prevents this; a split-tunnel or partial configuration may not.
Why does WebRTC need my addresses at all?
Because it connects peers directly rather than through a server. To find a path through routers and firewalls, each side gathers every address it might be reachable at — local interfaces, the public address a STUN server reports, and relay candidates — and the two sides try combinations until one works. Collecting addresses is the mechanism, not a flaw.
Should I disable WebRTC?
Probably not outright, since it breaks video calling and screen sharing. The targeted measures are better: keep mDNS obfuscation enabled, use a VPN configured to route and block everything outside the tunnel, and if you need stronger isolation, use a separate browser or profile with WebRTC disabled for sensitive work.
Does this test contact a server?
It may. Discovering a public address requires asking an external STUN server, so a full test makes that request by design — that is what a public candidate means. Local and mDNS candidates are gathered without any network request, and nothing about your results is sent to BitDark.
