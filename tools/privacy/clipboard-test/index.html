⚠️ Limitations & Disclaimer

This tests one page, not your whole browser or any other site. It shows how your browser responds when this page asks to read the clipboard — silently, after a prompt, or not at all. It cannot tell you whether some other site has read your clipboard, because browsers deliberately isolate pages from one another and provide no way to audit another origin. A clean result here is not a guarantee that nothing anywhere has accessed your clipboard. Because the test may read your clipboard if you permit it, clear anything sensitive before running it. Everything stays in your browser; nothing is transmitted or stored. Results are provided as-is; BitDark.net is not liable for decisions made from them.

What This Tool Does

Click the button and the page calls the Clipboard API read method, then reports what happened: the browser handed the content over without comment, it prompted you first, or it refused. That one observation is more informative than it sounds, because it tells you which side of the permission boundary your browser puts silent clipboard reads on — and browsers differ.

What it is not is a snooping detector. Nothing in a web page can observe another origin reading your clipboard. If a tool claims to detect third-party clipboard access, it is describing something the web platform does not expose.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Copy something harmless first — a word from this page will do. Do not run the test with a password, recovery phrase or private message sitting on your clipboard.
  2. Click in this page so it has focus. Several browsers refuse clipboard reads for a document that is not focused, and a denial caused by focus looks the same as one caused by policy.
  3. Click Request Clipboard Read API. Watch for a permission prompt as well as the result; whether you were asked is the interesting part.
  4. Read the diagnostic output. Granted silently, granted after a prompt, and denied are three different answers about your browser, and each is a reasonable outcome.
  5. Try the same test in another browser. The differences between them are the clearest way to understand that this is policy, not a fixed rule of the web.
  6. Click Clear Diagnostics when you are done, which removes any clipboard content from the page.

Why Reads and Writes Are Treated Differently

Writing to the clipboard is broadly permitted when it follows a user gesture, because the damage is bounded: a page can put the wrong thing on your clipboard, which is deceptive but visible when you paste.

Reading is different. Your clipboard holds whatever you last copied, and people copy passwords out of managers, recovery phrases out of wallets, and private text out of messages. A page that could read it silently would be reading a rolling window of your most sensitive input. Browsers therefore require some combination of a permission grant, a focused document and a user gesture — and some do not offer silent reads at all. Paste is the alternative path, and it is safe precisely because you perform it deliberately.

The Permission States, and What Each One Means

Underneath the button, the browser is resolving a permission called clipboard-read, and it can land in one of three states. Knowing which you got is more useful than knowing whether the read succeeded.

StateWhat happenedWhat it tells you
grantedThe read returned without asking you.This origin has standing permission, or your browser grants reads following a user gesture without a prompt.
promptYou were asked, and your answer decided it.The browser defers to you per site. Declining here is the correct default for a site you do not already trust.
deniedThe read failed with no prompt.Either you refused earlier and the choice was remembered, or this browser does not offer silent reads to pages at all.

A fourth outcome looks like failure but is not: the read can reject because the document was not focused, because the context is not secure, or because the browser did not associate the call with a user gesture. These are conditions on the call rather than decisions about permission, which is why clicking into the page before testing matters — otherwise you can conclude your browser refuses clipboard reads when it would have allowed one.

Browser families differ here in a way no specification settles. Some implement the permission and remember your answer per origin; some prompt every time; at least one does not expose a silent read path to ordinary pages at all, so the only way content reaches a page is you pressing paste. All three are defensible positions, which is exactly why testing your own browser beats assuming.

What Clipboard Attacks Actually Look Like

The attack that happens in volume is clipboard replacement against cryptocurrency users. Malware on the device watches for a string that looks like a wallet address and swaps in the attacker's. The victim copies the right address, pastes the wrong one, and confirms a transfer that cannot be reversed. This runs on the device, not in a web page, so browser clipboard policy does not stop it.

The defence is to verify after pasting rather than trusting the copy: check the first and last several characters of an address against the source before confirming anything irreversible. A second common trick is pastejacking, where a page intercepts a copy and substitutes different text — the reason to paste commands into a plain editor first rather than straight into a terminal.

A Worked Example You Can Reproduce

Copy a short distinctive word, click into this page, and run the test. Note exactly what happened — prompt or no prompt, granted or denied. Then open the same page in a different browser and repeat with the same clipboard content. If the two disagree, you have just demonstrated the point: clipboard read access is a per-browser policy decision, not a property of the web, and a result from one browser tells you nothing about another. Finish with Clear Diagnostics, and copy something harmless over your test content.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this detect whether a site is snooping on my clipboard?
No, and no page can. This demonstrates the permission model on this page only: it shows whether your browser grants a clipboard read, and whether that happened silently or after a prompt. A third-party script reading your clipboard on some other site is invisible from here, because browsers isolate pages from one another.
Why can a page write to my clipboard more easily than read from it?
Because the risks are not symmetric. Writing can be annoying or deceptive — pasting an address that is not the one you copied — but reading exposes whatever you last copied, which is routinely a password, a recovery phrase or a private message. Browsers therefore allow writes in response to a user gesture and gate reads behind permission, a focused document, or in some browsers no silent access at all.
Why did nothing happen when I clicked the button?
Several normal reasons. The page may not have focus, the request may need a user gesture the browser did not associate with the click, the context may not be secure, or your browser may simply not implement silent clipboard reads. A denial here is the permission model working, not a fault.
Is clipboard hijacking a real attack?
Yes, mostly on the write side and mostly against cryptocurrency users. Malware that watches for a wallet address on the clipboard and replaces it with the attacker's is a long-running class of attack, and it usually runs on the device rather than in a web page. Checking what you actually pasted, character by character, before confirming a transfer is the defence that works.
Does this tool read or store what I copied?
It reads your clipboard only if you click the request button and your browser grants it, and what comes back is displayed on this page and nowhere else. Nothing is transmitted and nothing is stored. Do not run the test with something sensitive on your clipboard — not because of this page, but because it is a good habit.
