⚠️ Limitations & Disclaimer

Hiding is not protecting. Least-significant-bit steganography conceals the existence of a message; it does nothing to secure its contents. Anyone who extracts the low bits in the same order reads your text in plain form, and the method is public. If the content matters, encrypt it with a real tool first and hide the ciphertext. Two practical limits follow: the output must stay a lossless PNG, because JPEG compression and most platform re-encoding destroy the low bits and with them the message; and statistical steganalysis can frequently detect that an image carries a payload, particularly when the payload is large relative to the image or when the original is available for comparison. What This Tool Does

Encoding takes an image and a block of text, writes the text's bits into the lowest bit of the pixel colour values, and exports the result as a PNG that looks identical to the original. Extraction does the reverse: it reads those low bits back and reassembles the message.

It works because the bottom bit of a colour value is beneath the threshold of human vision. Changing a channel from 200 to 201 is a difference of one part in 256, invisible on any display and indistinguishable from sensor noise in a photograph.

How to Use the Tool

  1. To hide a message: load a cover image, type your text into Secret Message to Embed, and click Encode Secret.
  2. Use a photograph as the cover, not a flat graphic or a logo. Natural images have noisy low bits already, so an embedded payload blends in; a poster of solid colour blocks has uniform low bits where any modification stands out immediately under analysis.
  3. Click Embed & Download PNG to save the result. PNG is required, and converting the file to JPEG afterwards destroys the message.
  4. To recover a message: load the stego image and click Extract / Decode or Extract Payload. The recovered text appears in Extracted Secret Message, and Copy Text takes it.
  5. Verify before you rely on it. Download the stego image, reload that downloaded file, and extract. Round-tripping through an actual file is the only check that proves the export survived.
  6. Click Clear to remove the image and text from the page.

How the Bits Are Stored

Each pixel holds colour channels as values from 0 to 255 — eight bits each. The highest bit contributes 128 to the value and matters enormously; the lowest contributes 1 and is effectively noise. Replacing that lowest bit changes the channel by at most one.

So the text is converted to bits and written one bit per channel across the image in order. Three bits per pixel means a one-megapixel image can carry about three million bits in principle. Reading back is the same walk in the same order, collecting the low bits and reassembling bytes — which is also why both sides must agree on the ordering, and why a stego image produced by one tool generally does not decode in another.

Why Lossless Formats Are Mandatory

PNG compresses losslessly: every pixel value you wrote comes back exactly. JPEG compresses by discarding detail the eye is unlikely to miss — and the information it discards first is precisely the fine, high-frequency variation that the low bits represent.

The practical consequence catches people out constantly. Embed a message, upload the PNG to a service that re-encodes images for bandwidth, and the message is gone. Messaging apps, social platforms and content delivery networks all do this routinely. If the image will pass through anything that might re-encode it, assume the payload will not survive, and send the file directly as a file.

How Hidden Data Is Detected

In a natural photograph the low bits are not uniformly random — they follow patterns from the sensor and the processing pipeline. Embedded data replaces them with a different distribution, and statistical tests pick that up. Text is especially visible, because ASCII leaves regular structure in the bit stream.

Detection gets easier as the payload grows: an image filled to capacity looks very different from an untouched one. And if an analyst has the original image, comparison is immediate and conclusive. The way to make detection harder is to use a small fraction of the capacity in a noisy photograph, and to never publish the cover image elsewhere.

A Worked Example You Can Reproduce

Load a photograph, embed a short sentence, and download the PNG. Open the original and the stego image side by side — no visible difference, as expected. Reload the downloaded PNG into the tool and extract: your sentence comes back. Now convert that PNG to JPEG with any image editor, reload the JPEG, and extract again: the output is garbage, because the compression discarded the bits that held the message. That single comparison explains the format requirement better than any description of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a hidden message encrypted?
No. LSB embedding hides the bits; it does not protect them. Anyone who extracts the low bits in the same order reads your text directly. If the content matters, encrypt it first with a real tool and hide the ciphertext — then extraction yields an unreadable blob rather than your message.
Why must the output be PNG?
Because PNG compression is lossless, so every pixel value survives exactly. JPEG discards information to save space, and the low bits are the first thing it throws away. Save a stego image as JPEG, or let a platform re-encode it, and the hidden data is destroyed.
Can anyone tell a message is hidden?
Often, yes. Statistical steganalysis looks at the distribution of low bits, and embedded data — especially text — leaves a detectable signature, more so as the payload grows relative to the image. Comparing against the original image makes it trivial. Treat this as concealment from casual inspection, not from analysis.
How much text fits in an image?
It depends on the pixel count and how many bits per channel are used. At one bit per colour channel, each pixel carries three bits, so a one-megapixel image holds roughly three million bits — a few hundred kilobytes of text in principle. Using far less than the capacity is wiser, because a nearly full image is much easier to detect.
Is my image or message uploaded?
No. The image is read into a canvas in your browser, pixels are modified there, and the result is exported locally. Nothing is transmitted at any point.
