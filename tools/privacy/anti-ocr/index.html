What This Tool Does

Type text into the payload box and the tool draws it onto a canvas with deliberate visual interference: random speckle, displaced and rotated letters, and thin grids or slits cutting across the strokes. The result is exported as a PNG. A person reading it sees slightly scruffy text; a character recognition engine sees glyph shapes that no longer match the clean forms it was trained to segment.

Four visual themes change the look rather than the technique. The sliders are where the real behaviour lives, and the preview updates so you can find the point where machines struggle and people still manage.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Enter your text in Sensitive Text Payload (To Obfuscate). Keep it short — the distortion that defeats recognition also slows human reading, and long passages become tiring fast.
  2. Pick a theme under Visual Camouflage Theme: Atom Terminal, Phosphor Amber CRT, Cyberpunk High-Frequency Inversion or Subliminal Grain / Microprint. This is presentation, not strength.
  3. Set the three sliders — Adversarial Speckle Density, Interference Grids / Slits and Letter Displacement & Jitter. Raise jitter first: in the measurements below it is the only control whose effect is predictable. Do not push the grid slider to its maximum, which measured worse than its middle settings.
  4. Watch the preview in Adversarial Canvas Preview and stop as soon as you personally start struggling. Past that point you are mostly hurting your readers.
  5. Click Regenerate Adversarial Patterns for a different random seed at the same settings. Two renders of identical text look different, which is useful if you are publishing several pieces.
  6. Export Image (PNG) saves the canvas, or Copy to Clipboard puts it straight into another application.

Why Noise Degrades OCR

A traditional recognition pipeline binarises the image, segments it into connected regions it believes are characters, and matches each region against learned glyph shapes. Every stage makes assumptions: that characters are separable, that strokes are continuous, that baselines are straight.

Speckle attacks binarisation by adding pixels that survive thresholding. Interference grids attack segmentation by connecting adjacent characters or splitting single ones. Jitter attacks matching by rotating and displacing glyphs away from the canonical forms. None of these is exotic; they simply violate what the pipeline expects, and classic engines degrade quickly as a result.

Where It Stops Working

Modern recognition is largely neural, trained on enormous quantities of degraded real-world images — photographs at an angle, poor lighting, creased paper, low resolution. Robustness to noise is the point of that training, so the same distortion that collapses a classic engine may barely dent a current one.

Worse, the distortion is systematic. If someone cares enough, they can characterise the pattern and filter it — a median filter removes isolated speckle, frequency-domain work removes regular grids. Anything generated by a public tool has that weakness by definition. So the honest framing is cost, not prevention: you are making bulk automated harvesting more expensive, and that is all.

When It Is Worth Using

The realistic use is content you are publishing in a place that is scraped indiscriminately, where you want to raise the cost of automated collection without hiding the content from humans — a contact detail on a public page, a short notice you would rather not see aggregated into a dataset. Against a determined individual it buys nothing. Against a crawler that runs a stock recognition pipeline across millions of pages and does not stop to characterise your particular noise pattern, it often does.

Weigh that against the cost every time. The moment text becomes an image it leaves the accessible web: no screen reader, no translation, no text search, no reflow on a small screen, no copy and paste. If the content matters to readers, publish it as text and accept the scraping, or publish both.

Three things follow. The defaults work against this engine: the secret was not recovered in two of the three modes. Jitter is the only dependable control — its effect is monotonic and identical in all three modes, which means it degrades character recognition itself rather than confusing the layout step. The maximum grid setting is a trap. At 50 the text was read almost perfectly, while the same slider at 10 or 25 did real damage; the dense regular bars the maximum produces are exactly the kind of periodic rule that OCR preprocessing is built to strip out, whereas sparser irregular interference survives it.

The wide spread between the three columns for speckle and mid-range grid is the most important caveat on this whole page. Those settings mostly disrupt layout analysis, so how much they help depends on how the recogniser was invoked — something you cannot control and will not know. A scraper that crops to the text first bypasses that disruption entirely. Only the jitter column is stable across all three, which is why it deserves the first slider you move. And all of this is one engine on one line of text: a different engine, and especially a modern neural recogniser, may produce quite different numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this reliably defeat OCR?
No, and you should not plan around it doing so. Classic OCR engines rely on clean glyph shapes, so speckle, jitter and interference grids degrade them noticeably. Modern machine-learning recognisers are far more tolerant of noise, and an attacker who knows what the distortion looks like can often pre-process it away. Treat the output as friction, not protection.
Is the text still in the image somewhere as text?
No. The tool draws your text onto a canvas as pixels and exports that canvas as a PNG. There is no text layer and no metadata copy of the string, so a text search of the file finds nothing. That is also the downside — nobody can select, copy or search it either.
Why does the output hurt accessibility?
Because a screen reader cannot read pixels. Anything you convert here becomes invisible to assistive technology, to translation tools and to text search. If other people need to read the content, do not use this on it, or publish an accessible version alongside.
Which settings have the biggest effect?
We measured this rather than guessing. Letter displacement is the only control with a predictable effect: accuracy fell steadily as it rose, in every segmentation mode we tested. Speckle helped but inconsistently. The surprise was the interference grid — at its maximum setting the text was read almost perfectly, noticeably worse than the same slider at its middle values. The measured table in the article has the figures.
Is my text uploaded anywhere?
No. The text is rendered to a canvas in your browser and exported from there. Nothing is transmitted. Copy to Clipboard and Export Image (PNG) both operate locally on the canvas you can see.
