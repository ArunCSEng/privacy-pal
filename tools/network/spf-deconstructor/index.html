⚠️ Limitations & Disclaimer

This reads the record you paste; it does not resolve DNS. It parses the text into mechanisms and qualifiers, explains each one, and counts the DNS-querying mechanisms against the RFC 7208 limit. Because it performs no lookups, it cannot expand an include to count what that record spends in turn \u2014 so a record that looks comfortably under ten here can still exceed it once a provider's own includes are resolved. It also cannot tell you whether the hosts an a or mx mechanism points at are the ones actually sending your mail. Confirm against a resolving validator before publishing a change, and remember that SPF alone does not stop spoofing of the visible From address \u2014 that is what DMARC alignment is for. What This Tool Does

An SPF record is a single line of DNS text that decides which servers may send mail for your domain — and it is unusually easy to get wrong in ways that produce no error until mail starts failing. Paste a record here and it is broken into its individual mechanisms, each explained in plain terms, with a running count of the DNS lookups an evaluating server would have to make. That count is the part most people have never checked, and it is the most common cause of a record that quietly stops working.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Paste your record — the full TXT value starting with v=spf1. You can retrieve your current one with dig +short TXT example.com or any DNS lookup tool, then paste the SPF line here.
  2. Or start from a preset — Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 load typical single-provider records, Complex Enterprise Hybrid shows a realistic multi-vendor record where the lookup count becomes a real concern, and Over-Permissive (+all) demonstrates the one configuration you should never publish.
  3. Click Deconstruct SPF — the record is split into one row per mechanism.
  4. Read the table left to right — Qualifier is the pass, fail, softfail or neutral prefix; Mechanism is the type of match; Value / Scope is what it authorises; DNS Cost is what that mechanism charges against the limit; and Operational Intent explains in words what the row actually does.
  5. Check the total lookup count first — this is the number that matters. At or near ten, your record is fragile, and the next vendor you add will break it.
  6. Check the final all qualifier — it governs everything not matched above it, so it determines what the record does in practice.

If the count is already high, look for include mechanisms belonging to services you no longer use — stale vendor includes are the usual reason a record has crept over the limit.

Mechanisms and What They Cost

An evaluator reads the mechanisms left to right and stops at the first match. The cost column matters because each DNS-querying mechanism counts toward a hard ceiling:

MechanismMatchesDNS cost
ip4 / ip6A literal address or CIDR range0 — written in the record itself
includeEverything another domain's SPF record authorises1, plus whatever that record spends
aThe A/AAAA records of a domain1
mxThe hosts listed as the domain's mail exchangers1
existsWhether a constructed name resolves at all1
ptrReverse DNS of the sending IP1 — discouraged; slow and unreliable
allEverything not already matched0

The Ten-Lookup Limit

RFC 7208 caps an evaluation at ten DNS-querying mechanisms. Go over it and the result is PermError, which receivers generally treat as a failure — so the symptom of exceeding the limit is not a warning anywhere, it is mail from legitimate senders starting to fail authentication.

The trap is that include is recursive. Adding include:_spf.example-vendor.com costs one lookup for the include itself, plus every lookup inside that vendor's record — which you do not control and which can grow when the vendor reorganises. A record with four or five includes can be two vendors away from breaking without a single character of your own changing.

The standard remedies are to drop includes for services you no longer use, replace a small vendor's include with explicit ip4 ranges where they publish stable addresses (those cost nothing), and avoid ptr entirely.

Qualifiers: What Happens on a Match

Each mechanism carries an optional prefix. + is pass and is the default when none is written. - is hardfail, asking the receiver to reject. ~ is softfail, meaning suspicious but usually still delivered. ? is neutral, which says nothing at all.

In practice the qualifier that matters most is the one on all, because it catches every sender not matched earlier. ~all is the right place to start while you confirm your legitimate senders are covered; -all is the goal once they are. +all authorises the entire internet to send as your domain and is strictly worse than having no record.

A Worked Example You Can Reproduce

Load Complex Enterprise Hybrid and look at the cost column rather than the mechanisms. The ip4 and ip6 entries contribute nothing, while each include and the mx add one apiece, and the running total climbs toward the ceiling. Then load Over-Permissive (+all): it costs almost no lookups and is nonetheless the most dangerous record of the set, because the final qualifier passes everything. The two together make the point that a record's safety and its lookup cost are entirely separate problems — a cheap record can be wide open, and a careful one can still break by being expensive.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the ten-lookup limit, and why does it break SPF?
RFC 7208 allows an SPF evaluation at most ten DNS-querying mechanisms. Each include, a, mx, ptr and exists costs one, and an include pulls in whatever that record spends too. Exceed ten and the result is a PermError, which most receivers treat as a failure — so adding one more vendor can silently break authentication for all of your mail.
Which mechanisms cost a DNS lookup?
include, a, mx, ptr and exists each cost one, and the cost of an include is recursive. ip4 and ip6 cost nothing at all, because the address is written directly in the record. That is why replacing an include with explicit ip4 ranges is the standard way to get back under the limit.
What is the difference between ~all and -all?
The qualifier before all decides what happens to mail from a sender not otherwise matched. ~all is softfail, meaning the message is suspicious but should usually still be delivered; -all is hardfail, asking receivers to reject it. Start at ~all while you confirm your senders, then move to -all. +all passes everything and should never be published.
Why is +all dangerous?
It tells every receiver that any server on the internet is authorised to send mail using your domain. That is worse than publishing no SPF record at all, because it actively vouches for forgeries. If you see it, it is almost always a mistake left behind during testing.
Is my record uploaded anywhere?
No. The record is parsed in your browser and nothing is sent to a server. The tool also performs no DNS resolution — it counts the lookups an evaluator would make from the text of the record, rather than making them, so an include's own contents are counted but not fetched.
