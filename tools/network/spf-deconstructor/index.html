The Engineering Architecture of Sender Policy Framework (RFC 7208)

The Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP), originally formalized in RFC 821 and modernized under RFC 5321, was engineered during an era when the ARPANET comprised an insular community of trusted academic and military computer operators. Consequently, SMTP contains zero native authentication mechanisms. Any host connected to the internet can initiate an SMTP handshake with a Mail Transfer Agent (MTA) and specify arbitrary administrative domain identities in the MAIL FROM: (envelope sender / bounce address) and From: (message header) fields.

This intrinsic architectural trust vacuum facilitated decades of industrial-scale business email compromise (BEC), phishing, display-name spoofing, and spam delivery. Published in April 2014, RFC 7208: Sender Policy Framework for Authorizing Use of Domains in Email, Version 1 established an administrative authorization layer. SPF empowers a domain owner to publish a public DNS TXT record declaring exactly which outbound MTAs, IP addresses, and third-party relay services are authorized to dispatch emails under its envelope identity.

During an SMTP transaction, when an outbound server issues the MAIL FROM:<sender@domain.com> envelope command, the receiving MTA extracts the domain portion, queries the domain's authoritative DNS servers for TXT records starting with v=spf1, and executes the compiled rule tree against the connecting IP address. The resulting verdict—Pass, Fail, SoftFail, Neutral, None, PermError, or TempError—dictates downstream spam scoring and DMARC alignment evaluation.

The Mathematical Architecture of RFC 7208 DNS Lookup Limits

The most notorious engineering constraint in email authentication is the 10-DNS-lookup limit codified in RFC 7208 §4.6.4:

"SPF implementations MUST limit the number of mechanisms and modifiers that do DNS lookups to at most 10 per SPF evaluation, taking into account any 'include' mechanisms, 'redirect' modifiers, and 'exp' modifiers... If this limit is exceeded, the evaluation MUST return a 'permerror'."

1. Why Does the 10-Lookup Ceiling Exist?

The restriction was introduced to prevent DNS amplification and denial-of-service (DoS) attacks against mail receivers. If RFC 7208 permitted infinite lookups, an adversary could forge emails referencing a malicious domain whose SPF record contained nested include: directives referencing hundreds of external domains, triggering an explosive cascade of recursive DNS queries for every inbound email received.

2. The Cost Accounting Matrix: What Counts Toward the Limit?

Understanding exactly which mechanisms consume DNS lookups is essential for managing your organization's query budget:

Mechanism / Modifier Consumes DNS Budget? Underlying Query Type Operational Function
include:<domain> Yes (+1 + Nested) TXT Recursively queries external domain's SPF record and evaluates its contents.
a / a:<domain> Yes (+1) A / AAAA Queries forward IP address(es) for target or current domain.
mx / mx:<domain> Yes (+1) MX, then A/AAAA Resolves MX hostnames for domain, then resolves their corresponding IPs.
ptr / ptr:<domain> Yes (+1) PTR + Forward A DEPRECATED: Triggers reverse pointer resolution on client IP.
exists:<domain> Yes (+1) A Checks whether target domain resolves to any IPv4 address (common in macro routing).
redirect=<domain> Yes (+1) TXT Transfers policy evaluation entirely to target domain's SPF record.
ip4:<cidr> No (0) None (In-Memory) Evaluates connecting IPv4 address directly against CIDR mask in RAM.
ip6:<cidr> No (0) None (In-Memory) Evaluates connecting IPv6 address directly against 128-bit mask in RAM.
all No (0) None (In-Memory) Terminal catch-all evaluator applying matching qualifier to all senders.
exp=<domain> No (0) (Conditional) TXT Does not count toward 10-limit during primary evaluation; queried only upon HardFail.

3. The Void Lookup Limit (RFC 7208 §4.6.4)

In addition to the global 10-lookup ceiling, RFC 7208 establishes a secondary boundary: the void lookup limit. If a mechanism queries DNS and receives an answer containing NXDOMAIN (RCODE 3) or NODATA (an answer section with zero resource records), it is counted as a "void lookup". If the total count of void lookups reaches two (2) across the evaluation chain, the receiving MTA must abort with a PermError. This defends against DNS cache-poisoning probing and phantom subdomain attacks.

The Four Qualifiers: Mechanics & Enforcement

Every SPF mechanism can be prefixed with an operational qualifier that instructs the receiving mail server what verdict to emit when a sending IP matches that mechanism:

  • + (Pass): The default qualifier. If no prefix is specified (e.g., include:_spf.google.com), + is implicitly applied. An IP matching this mechanism is authenticated and authorized.
  • - (Fail / HardFail): Explicit authorization denial. Senders matching this rule are unauthorized. The receiving MTA is instructed to reject the message outright during the SMTP RCPT TO or DATA phase.
  • ~ (SoftFail): Administrative transition qualifier. Senders matching this rule are unauthorized, but the MTA should accept the email and append an Authentication-Results header marking it as suspicious or forward it to junk/spam folders. When paired with DMARC p=reject, a SoftFail results in full message rejection.
  • ? (Neutral): No assertion made. The domain owner explicitly states that no policy determination is possible. Treated identically to a missing SPF record.

Worked Example: Enterprise SPF Anatomy & DNS Budgeting

Let us trace how an enterprise SPF record is parsed and how its DNS lookup budget is calculated: 

v=spf1 mx include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net ip4:198.51.100.0/24 ip6:2001:db8::/32 -all

Step-by-Step Lexical Execution Trace:

  1. Token 0 (v=spf1): Protocol Version identifier. Identifies the record as an RFC 7208 declaration. Consumes 0 lookups.
  2. Token 1 (mx): Evaluates to +mx (default Pass). Direct DNS cost: +1. At runtime, the receiver queries the MX records of the domain, then resolves their A/AAAA records to assemble an authorized IP whitelist.
  3. Token 2 (include:_spf.google.com): Evaluates to +include:_spf.google.com. Direct DNS cost: +1. The receiver queries the TXT record of _spf.google.com. Inside Google's record are further nested includes (e.g., _netblocks.google.com, _netblocks2.google.com, _netblocks3.google.com), which consume an additional 3 lookups.
  4. Token 3 (include:sendgrid.net): Evaluates to +include:sendgrid.net. Direct DNS cost: +1. The receiver queries sendgrid.net's TXT record, consuming 1 lookup.
  5. Token 4 (ip4:198.51.100.0/24): Evaluates to +ip4:198.51.100.0/24. Direct DNS cost: 0. Evaluated in local memory. Matches any client IP in the range 198.51.100.0 through 198.51.100.255.
  6. Token 5 (ip6:2001:db8::/32): Evaluates to +ip6:2001:db8::/32. Direct DNS cost: 0. Evaluated in local memory.
  7. Token 6 (-all): Terminal Catch-All. Direct DNS cost: 0. Enforces HardFail on all connecting IPs that failed to match prior mechanisms.

Cumulative DNS Overhead: In this configuration, direct lookups count as 3 of 10. However, taking recursive nested includes into account (Google adds 3 subsequent lookups), the total evaluation chain consumes 6 of 10 lookups—safely within RFC compliance thresholds.

The SPF Flattening Strategy: Architecture, Tradeoffs & Automation

When growing enterprise infrastructure exceeds the 10-DNS-lookup barrier, organizations face two choices: restructure their mail topology across subdomains, or implement SPF flattening.

1. What is SPF Flattening?

Flattening is the process of recursively resolving all domains specified in include:, a, and mx mechanisms down to their ultimate, static ip4: and ip6: CIDR blocks, replacing lookup-heavy mechanisms with direct IP definitions: 

// Unflattened (Consumes 7 DNS Lookups):
v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net include:mailgun.org ~all

// Fully Flattened (Consumes 0 DNS Lookups):
v=spf1 ip4:35.190.247.0/24 ip4:64.233.160.0/19 ip4:66.102.0.0/20 ip4:167.89.0.0/17 ip4:198.61.254.0/23 ip6:2001:4860:4000::/36 ~all

2. The Fragility of Manual Flattening

While flattening eliminates DNS lookup costs, manual flattening is exceptionally dangerous. Cloud email providers (such as Google Workspace and Microsoft 365) continually expand, migrate, and decommission IP subnets across their global edge networks. If an administrator flattens include:_spf.google.com into static IP blocks in January, and Google provisions a new data center subnet in March, outbound emails routed through those new servers will immediately fail SPF.

Engineering Principle: If an organization must flatten SPF records, it must deploy an automated DNS management pipeline or dynamic flattening service that polls vendor SPF records via automated crons every 15 minutes, re-computes subnets, and updates DNS zone apex records dynamically via DNS APIs.

Common Mistakes and Catastrophic Failures in SPF Deployment

Even minor syntax oversights can compromise your domain's email deliverability or expose it to impersonation:

  • The +all Open Relay Disaster: Writing +all instead of -all or ~all is a catastrophic misconfiguration. The +all directive explicitly instructs the entire internet that every mail server on Earth is authorized to send email on behalf of your domain, rendering spoofing defenses completely useless.
  • Publishing Multiple SPF TXT Records: Merging records incorrectly often results in two discrete TXT entries in the DNS zone: 
    example.com. IN TXT "v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all"
example.com. IN TXT "v=spf1 include:sendgrid.net ~all"
    According to RFC 7208 §3.2, receiving mail servers must treat multiple SPF records as a fatal syntax error, instantly returning a PermError and refusing to authenticate either service.
  • Directives Placed After the all Terminator: The all mechanism is an unconditional catch-all. Compliant MTAs terminate parsing the moment they hit all. Any directives placed after it (e.g., v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com -all ip4:198.51.100.1) will never be reached or evaluated.
  • Over-Reliance on the mx Mechanism: Many administrators habitually include the mx directive. However, MX records designate inbound mail exchangers. Many organizations use distinct cloud infrastructure for outbound email (e.g., Google Workspace) while running specialized spam filters for inbound routing (e.g., Proofpoint, Mimecast). Adding mx adds unnecessary DNS queries while authorizing servers that may never transmit outbound mail.

Frequently Asked Technical Questions

What is the difference between an SPF PermError and a TempError?
A PermError (Permanent Error) occurs when the SPF record is fundamentally unparseable, contains invalid syntax, includes multiple SPF records on the same domain, or exceeds the 10-DNS-lookup or 2-void-lookup limits. Receiving MTAs typically reject the message or treat SPF as failed. A TempError (Temporary Error) occurs when a transient DNS network issue (such as a DNS server timeout or network unreachability) prevents resolution. Receiving MTAs usually defer delivery, requesting the sending MTA to retry later.
Why does email forwarding often break SPF authentication?
SPF authenticates the connecting IP address of the transmitting MTA against the domain declared in the envelope MAIL FROM address. When an email is forwarded (e.g., user@company.com forwards to user@gmail.com), the forwarding mail server connects to Gmail using its own IP address while retaining the original envelope sender. Because the forwarding server's IP is not in the original sender's SPF record, SPF evaluates to SoftFail or HardFail. This is why DKIM and ARC are crucial to preserve end-to-end authentication during forwarding.
Does the 255-character DNS TXT string limit restrict SPF records?
A single DNS character-string within a TXT record is limited to 255 octets by RFC 1035. However, a single DNS TXT resource record can contain multiple concatenated strings up to a total UDP payload limit of around 4096 bytes (using EDNS0). Compliant DNS nameservers and resolvers automatically concatenate multiple strings within a TXT record into a single seamless SPF string.
How does SPF interact with DMARC alignment?
DMARC requires that the domain in the visible message header (RFC 5322 From:) matches or aligns with the domain authenticated by SPF (RFC 5321 MAIL FROM). Under "relaxed" alignment (default), subdomains align with their organizational parent (e.g., mail.example.com aligns with example.com). Under "strict" alignment, an exact match is required. Even if SPF passes for a third-party relay like bounces.sendgrid.net, DMARC will fail unless SendGrid is configured with custom reverse path domain branding to achieve identifier alignment.
What is the purpose of the 'redirect=' modifier?
The redirect= modifier instructs the evaluating MTA to completely cease evaluating the current record and instead evaluate the SPF record of the specified target domain. Unlike include:, which returns execution back to the caller upon a non-match, redirect= replaces the caller's evaluation entirely and should only be placed at the very end of a record without an accompanying all mechanism.
Can I use both -all and ~all in different parts of my record?
No. The all mechanism is designed strictly as a terminal catch-all for unmatched IP addresses. Having multiple all mechanisms creates syntactic ambiguity and violates RFC 7208 specifications, leading to unpredictable parsing behavior or an immediate PermError on receiving MTAs.

No strings, subnets, or domain names are transmitted across the network, logged to analytics servers, or sent to backend APIs. You can disconnect your device from the internet or inspect your browser's Network tab to confirm zero bytes of network egress.
What should I do if my SPF record requires more than 10 lookups?
You have three architecturally sound options: (1) Delegate distinct email streams to dedicated subdomains (e.g., marketing.company.com, billing.company.com) so each has its own independent 10-lookup budget; (2) Audit your record to remove obsolete includes, redundant mx mechanisms, and unused SaaS vendors; or (3) Implement an automated, dynamically refreshed SPF flattening service.
