Understanding Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) and Autonomous Systems

The global Internet is not a monolithic network. Instead, it is an intricate federation of more than 100,000 heterogeneous, independently administered networks known as Autonomous Systems (AS). An Autonomous System represents a connected group of Internet Protocol (IP) routing prefixes operated under the control of a single administrative entity—such as an Internet Service Provider (ISP), multinational enterprise, cloud hyperscaler, or academic institution—that presents a unified, coherent routing policy to the global routing table.

The glue that binds these tens of thousands of disparate networks together is the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP), specifically BGP version 4 (BGP-4) defined in RFC 4271. Unlike Interior Gateway Protocols (IGPs) such as OSPF, IS-IS, or EIGRP—which operate within the trusted perimeter of a single organization to optimize packet delivery based on link metrics, delays, and bandwidth—BGP is an Exterior Gateway Protocol (EGP) engineered for policy-driven inter-domain routing.

At the inter-domain level, shortest-path calculation is not merely a matter of milliseconds or link speed. It is fundamentally governed by commercial contracts, peering agreements, geographic jurisdiction, regulatory boundaries, and transit costs. BGP enables network operators to translate complex economic relationships into mathematical path attributes that control exactly where, how, and through whom billions of gigabytes of traffic traverse the globe every second.

BGP Path Vector Protocol Architecture & The AS_PATH Attribute

BGP is classified mathematically as a path-vector routing protocol. While distance-vector protocols (like RIP) only know the distance (hop count) and vector (next hop) to a destination, a path-vector protocol maintains the entire explicit sequence of Autonomous Systems that an advertisement has traversed.

This sequence is encapsulated within the AS_PATH attribute—one of BGP's four well-known mandatory attributes (along with ORIGIN, NEXT_HOP, and LOCAL_PREF in internal BGP).

1. Directionality and the Anatomy of an AS_PATH

One of the most frequent points of confusion among junior network engineers is the directionality of the AS_PATH attribute. BGP route advertisements travel in the opposite direction of the actual user data packets: 

Route Advertisement Propagation (Control Plane):
Origin AS (AS 15169 - Google)  ----->  Transit AS (AS 174 - Cogent)  ----->  Eyeball AS (AS 7018 - AT&T)

Resulting AS_PATH seen at AT&T edge router:
[ 174, 15169 ]

Actual User Data Packet Flow (Data Plane):
User (inside AS 7018)  =====>  Forwarded to AS 174  =====>  Delivered to AS 15169

In standard BGP representation:

  • The Origin AS is located at the far right of the sequence. This is the autonomous system that originally generated the network prefix advertisement via a network statement or BGP redistribution.
  • The Immediate Peer AS is located at the far left of the sequence. This is the adjacent autonomous system from which your border router directly received the BGP update.
  • Every intermediate AS prepends its own ASN to the left side of the list before relaying the update to external peers.

2. AS_SEQUENCE vs. AS_SET

Under RFC 4271, the AS_PATH attribute is technically composed of TLV (Type-Length-Value) segments. The two most prominent segment types are:

Segment Type RFC Specification Ordering Semantic Primary Operational Purpose
AS_SEQUENCE RFC 4271 Strictly Ordered List Standard eBGP propagation. Hops are recorded in exact sequential order as the route traverses boundary routers.
AS_SET RFC 4271 / RFC 6472 Unordered Mathematical Set Used during BGP route aggregation/summarization to preserve path history without false ordering. Deprecated by RFC 6472 due to BGPsec incompatibilities.

Global Internet Topology: The Tier Hierarchy and Peering Fabrics

Autonomous Systems do not exist on a flat plane. The global routing fabric is organized into an informal three-tier economic hierarchy:

1. Tier-1 Transit Backbones (Settlement-Free Peering)

At the pinnacle of the Internet sit roughly a dozen Tier-1 networks. A Tier-1 network is defined by a unique characteristic: it can reach every other network on the global Internet without paying for transit. Instead, Tier-1 providers connect to each other via settlement-free peering agreements across high-capacity inter-exchange facilities.

Prominent global Tier-1 backbones include:

  • AS3356: Lumen Technologies (formerly Level 3 / CenturyLink)
  • AS1299: Arelion (formerly Telia Carrier)
  • AS174: Cogent Communications
  • AS2914: NTT Communications
  • AS7018: AT&T Global Network
  • AS701: Verizon (formerly UUNET)
  • AS6453: Tata Communications
  • AS3257: GTT Communications

2. Tier-2 Networks (Regional Transits and Content Giants)

Tier-2 networks maintain extensive routing infrastructure and peer freely at Internet Exchange Points (IXPs) with many networks, but must still purchase commercial IP transit from one or more Tier-1 backbones to reach the entire global routing table. Hyperscale content providers (such as Google AS15169, Cloudflare AS13335, Meta AS32934, and Amazon AS16509) operate some of the largest global backbones in existence, but maintain selective peering policies while purchasing partial transit.

3. Tier-3 Networks (Access Providers and Enterprises)

Tier-3 networks are edge entities: local consumer ISPs, enterprise campus networks, and regional data centers. They purchase transit from upstream Tier-2 or Tier-1 providers and do not sell transit to third parties.

Traffic Engineering: AS-Path Prepending vs. Alternative Controls

One of the core challenges of multi-homed BGP networking is controlling inbound traffic. While outbound routing decisions can be strictly dictated using the LOCAL_PREF (Local Preference) attribute on internal routers, inbound traffic is decided by upstream networks over which you have no administrative authority.

The primary mechanism network administrators use to influence remote inbound routing is AS-Path Prepending.

1. The Mechanics of Prepending

During BGP best-path selection (RFC 4271), routers evaluate paths through a strict sequence of criteria:

  1. Highest Weight (Cisco proprietary, local to router)
  2. Highest Local Preference (Global across the local AS)
  3. Locally originated routes (via network or aggregate-address)
  4. Shortest AS_PATH length (evaluated by hop count)
  5. Lowest Origin Code (IGP < EGP < Incomplete)
  6. Lowest MED (Multi-Exit Discriminator)
  7. eBGP paths over iBGP paths
  8. Lowest IGP metric to the BGP next hop
  9. Oldest received route (for eBGP stability)
  10. Lowest BGP Router ID (tie-breaker)

Because step 4 evaluates total AS_PATH length, an organization operating AS 64512 can announce its prefix to an expensive backup ISP with artificial repetitions: 

Primary Link Announcement (to Arelion AS1299):
Prefix: 198.51.100.0/24  ===>  AS_PATH: [ 64512 ] (Length: 1 hop)

Backup Link Announcement (to Lumen AS3356):
Prefix: 198.51.100.0/24  ===>  AS_PATH: [ 64512, 64512, 64512 ] (Length: 3 hops)

When external autonomous systems compute their best path to 198.51.100.0/24, they will choose the 1-hop path via Arelion, leaving the 3-hop Lumen link dormant unless the primary circuit suffers a physical fiber cut.

Routing Control BGP Attribute Traffic Scope Propagation Range Determinism
AS-Path Prepending AS_PATH Inbound Global Internet Low-Medium (Can be overridden by upstream LocalPref)
BGP Communities COMMUNITY / EXT_COMMUNITY Inbound & Outbound Upstream Provider specific Very High (Directly adjusts provider LocalPref)
Local Preference LOCAL_PREF Outbound Internal AS only Absolute (Step 2 in BGP decision tree)
MED (Metric) MULTI_EXIT_DISC Inbound Directly adjacent peer AS only Medium (Ignored across different peer ASNs)

BGP Routing Loops and RFC 4271 Drop Semantics

The most vital operational responsibility of the AS_PATH attribute is loop prevention.

In an interconnected mesh network, an update announced by AS 65001 could potentially travel through AS 7018, then AS 2914, then AS 8075, and eventually be advertised back to an edge router of AS 65001. If AS 65001 accepted this update, a circular forwarding loop would be created, causing packets to bounce endlessly between routers until TTL exhaustion.

RFC 4271 Section 9.1.2: "If the Autonomous System number of the receiving BGP speaker is found in the AS_PATH attribute of the received UPDATE message, the message SHALL be rejected."

The BitDark visualizer checks for this condition automatically. If an Autonomous System number appears non-consecutively in an AS-Path sequence (e.g., 65001 -> 7018 -> 2914 -> 65001), the tool triggers a high-severity BGP Routing Loop Detected warning, highlighting the compromised links in dashed red contours.

Cryptographic Route Validation: RPKI, ROAs, and BGPsec

The traditional BGP protocol was designed in 1989 on an implicit model of mutual trust between network operators. Because plain BGP does not verify whether an Autonomous System actually owns the IP prefixes it advertises, the global Internet has suffered from catastrophic accidental leaks and malicious BGP Hijacks.

Modern defense against these vulnerabilities relies on Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI):

  • Route Origin Authorizations (ROAs): A ROA is a digitally signed cryptographic object published by an address space holder within an RIR repository. It explicitly states which ASN is authorized to originate a specific IP prefix, along with a MaxLength parameter defining the maximum permitted CIDR mask length.
  • Route Origin Validation (ROV): Border routers run local RPKI validators (such as Routinator or StayRTR) that download and verify ROA certificates. Received BGP routes are classified into three cryptographic states:
    • Valid: The route matches an authorized ASN and does not exceed MaxLength.
    • Invalid: The route originates from an unauthorized ASN or exceeds MaxLength (typically dropped immediately by tier-1 transits).
    • NotFound: No ROA exists for the prefix (routed via standard BGP heuristics).
  • BGPsec (RFC 8205): While RPKI ROV validates who originated a prefix, it does not validate the intermediate path. BGPsec extends RPKI by having every intermediate router cryptographically sign the AS_PATH hop using asymmetric keys, making unauthorized path modification mathematically impossible.

Historical BGP Catastrophes: When Path Attributes Fail

Understanding BGP mechanics requires reviewing the famous routing disasters that shaped modern defensive engineering:

1. The AS7007 Incident (1997)

In April 1997, a misconfigured router operated by MAI Network Services (AS7007) accidentally de-aggregated a vast portion of the global routing table into specific /24 prefixes and announced them to upstream providers. Because BGP prefers more-specific prefix lengths (longest prefix match) over shorter path lengths, traffic worldwide was sucked into AS7007's under-provisioned routers, causing widespread global outages. This incident catalyzed the adoption of strict prefix limits on peering sessions.

2. The Pakistan Telecom YouTube Hijack (2008)

In February 2008, Pakistan Telecom (AS17557) attempted to censor YouTube within its domestic borders by announcing a bogus 208.65.153.0/24 blackhole route. Due to an erroneous export filter, the route was leaked to upstream provider PCCW Global, which propagated the announcement worldwide. Within minutes, global traffic destined for YouTube was misdirected to Pakistan, taking YouTube offline globally for two hours. This catastrophe demonstrated the urgent necessity of RPKI Route Origin Validation.

3. The Allegro / Cloudflare Route Leak (2019)

In June 2019, a small regional ISP in Pennsylvania (DQE Communications) leaked internal BGP routes learned from Cloudflare to a transit provider (Allegro). Allegro forwarded these announcements to Verizon (AS701). Because Verizon accepted the more-specific routes without sanity-checking path lengths or capacities, a massive flood of Cloudflare traffic was shoved through local telecom lines in Pennsylvania, knocking large parts of the web offline.

Common Mistakes in BGP Engineering & AS-Path Management

When configuring border routers, engineers must guard against these standard operational pitfalls:

  • Private ASN Leakage: Failing to apply remove-private-as on external eBGP peering sessions. If private ASNs (e.g., 64512) leak into upstream transit providers, they degrade route quality and may be rejected by tier-1 prefix filters.
  • Excessive Prepending: Prepending your ASN 10 or 15 times to discourage traffic. Many major transit networks apply AS-Path Length Filtering that automatically drops any announcement exceeding 20 or 30 total hops to combat route flap damping and memory exhaustion.
  • Assuming Prepending Overrides Upstream LocalPref: Prepending only influences path length (Step 4 of BGP selection). If an upstream transit provider assigns a higher LOCAL_PREF to customer routes (Step 2), your 10 prepends will be completely ignored by that provider's internal routing engine.
  • Accidental Transitive Peering (Route Leaks): Forgetting to set no-export communities or failing to filter BGP updates between two transit providers. This inadvertently turns your enterprise network into an open, free transit backbone for the entire Internet.

Frequently Asked Technical Questions

What is the valid range for Autonomous System Numbers (ASNs)?
ASNs were originally 16-bit numbers ranging from 1 to 65535. RFC 6793 introduced 32-bit (4-byte) ASNs, extending the range up to 4,294,967,295. Public ASNs are assigned by IANA and Regional Internet Registries (ARIN, RIPE, APNIC, LACNIC, AFRINIC). Private ASNs (analogous to RFC 1918 private IP addresses) span 64512–65534 in 16-bit space and 4200000000–4294967294 in 32-bit space.
Why does BGP prefer shorter AS-Paths over faster physical routes?
BGP is an inter-domain policy protocol, not an interior performance optimizer. The global Internet is composed of independent commercial entities that do not share internal link metrics, latencies, or congestion states for competitive and security reasons. Path length serves as a robust, loop-free proxy for transit distance without exposing proprietary internal network telemetry.
How does BGP handle 4-byte ASNs when communicating with legacy 2-byte routers?
RFC 6793 defines backward compatibility mechanisms: when a modern 4-byte speaker advertises a path to a legacy 2-byte neighbor, it maps the 4-byte number to a reserved transitional ASN: AS23456 (known as AS_TRANS). The actual 4-byte path sequence is preserved in a transitive path attribute named AS4_PATH, allowing modern downstream routers to reconstruct the full 32-bit path without data loss.
What causes a BGP routing loop to occur in production?
Routing loops typically occur due to human misconfiguration: route redistribution loops between an Interior Gateway Protocol (such as OSPF) and BGP, stripping the AS_PATH attribute during redistribution, or improperly applying the as-override command on PE-CE links in MPLS VPNs. Standard eBGP routers automatically drop updates containing their own ASN to prevent forwarding loops.
Can AS-Path prepending guarantee that all inbound traffic uses my primary link?
No. Prepending makes a path appear less desirable during Step 4 of the BGP best-path algorithm. However, if an upstream ISP applies a high LOCAL_PREF (Step 2) to routes received from customer links (to earn revenue), that provider's routers will still prefer the prepended customer link over a shorter path received from a settlement-free peer.
What is BGP Route Flap Damping (RFD) and how does it relate to AS-Paths?
Route Flap Damping (RFC 2439) is a mechanism designed to protect router CPU and memory from unstable prefixes that repeatedly flap between up and down states. Each time a route flaps, a penalty is assessed. If an engineer excessively manipulates AS-Path prepends or flaps eBGP sessions, upstream providers may suppress (damp) the prefix for minutes or hours, causing connectivity blackouts.

Because it transmits zero outbound network packets, it does not query live RPKI cache validators.
