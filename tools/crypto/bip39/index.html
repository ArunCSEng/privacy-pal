A Seed Phrase Is the Wallet — Read This First

A BIP39 mnemonic isn't a password you can change; it is the private key material for every account derived from it. Anyone who sees the phrase can take the funds, and there is no reset. So the single most important rule is: never type a mnemonic that protects real funds into any website, including this one. A real wallet seed should be generated and kept on a dedicated hardware wallet or an offline, air-gapped device, never on a general-purpose browser that can run extensions, sync clipboards, or be screen-captured.

Treat this tool as a learning and testing utility: use it to understand how BIP39 works, to check the format of a phrase, or to work with test/empty wallets and public test vectors — not to create or inspect a seed that holds anything of value.

How BIP39 Works

BIP39 turns random entropy into a human-writable phrase. The steps are fixed and verifiable:

  1. Generate entropy — 128 to 256 bits of randomness.
  2. Add a checksum — the first (entropy ÷ 32) bits of its SHA-256 hash are appended.
  3. Split into 11-bit groups — each group (0–2047) indexes the 2048-word English wordlist.

224 bits7 bits21
256 bits8 bits24

Twelve words (128 bits) is already far beyond brute-force reach; 24 words is common for long-term storage. The checksum is why a single mistyped or swapped word is caught instead of silently producing a different wallet.

The Passphrase and the Seed

The optional passphrase (sometimes called the "25th word") is mixed in when the phrase is turned into the final seed. The seed is PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512 of the mnemonic, with the salt "mnemonic" + passphrase, run for 2048 iterations to produce a 512-bit seed. That seed is what a wallet feeds into BIP32 to derive keys and addresses.

Two consequences worth understanding: a different passphrase produces a completely different, equally valid wallet from the same words (useful, but if you forget it the funds are gone), and the passphrase is not stored in the phrase or checked by it — nothing can tell you whether you typed the right one.

How to Use the Tool

  1. Generate: pick a strength (12–24 words) and the tool produces a mnemonic with a valid checksum, along with its derived seed.
  2. Verify: paste a phrase to check the word count, that every word is in the BIP39 list, and that the checksum is valid — this catches typos and wrong word order.
  3. Add a passphrase to see how it changes the derived seed; the word count and checksum don't change, but the seed does.
  4. Only ever use test data — generated throwaway phrases or public test vectors, not a seed that holds funds.

Scope and Limits

  • Not for real seeds. As above — generate and store real wallet seeds on dedicated offline hardware, not in a browser.
  • Shows the BIP39 seed, not addresses. Turning the seed into coin-specific addresses is BIP32/BIP44 derivation, which this tool doesn't do.
  • Needs the official wordlist. The tool loads the 2048-word English list over the network; generation and verification won't run until it loads.
  • Runs locally otherwise. Entropy, checksum and seed derivation happen in your browser with Web Crypto; the phrase and passphrase aren't sent anywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to generate my real wallet seed here?
No. Never create or enter a seed that holds funds in a browser or on any website. Use a hardware wallet or an offline, air-gapped device. This tool is for learning and for test or empty wallets only, so the randomness never leaves a trustworthy environment.
What does the checksum actually protect against?
Typos and wrong word order. The last word encodes a checksum derived from the rest of the phrase, so a mistyped or transposed word almost always fails verification instead of silently pointing to a different wallet. It does not protect against someone who has the whole correct phrase.
What happens if I forget the passphrase?
The funds are unrecoverable. The passphrase isn't stored in the phrase or validated by the checksum, so there's no way to detect or recover a wrong or forgotten one. A different passphrase simply produces a different, equally valid wallet from the same words.
Is 12 words less secure than 24?
Both are secure against brute force — 12 words is 128 bits of entropy, already astronomically large. 24 words (256 bits) adds margin and is common for long-term cold storage, but 12-word phrases are standard and safe when generated with good randomness.
Why does the derived seed here not show my coin addresses?
This tool outputs the BIP39 master seed. Converting that into Bitcoin, Ethereum or other addresses uses BIP32/BIP44 hierarchical derivation paths, which is a separate step a full wallet performs. The seed shown is the input to that process.
